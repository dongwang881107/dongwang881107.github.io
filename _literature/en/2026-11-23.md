Suppose you want to estimate the resistance exerted by the vascular bed beyond an aortic branch from a short flow-MRI acquisition. Which measurements would help distinguish a change in that branch from a change in its neighbors? Miriam Löcke, Ahmed Attia, Dariusz Uciński and Cristóbal Bertoglio used the flow model to answer that question before choosing a sampling mask. In their simulated aorta, masks designed for the outlet resistances often lowered the estimation error at the same sampling budget, with the clearest advantage at five sampling locations per mask. [Journal record](https://doi.org/10.1088/1361-6420/aebb2c) · [Author preprint, Section 4, PDF pp. 13–23](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06712v1#page=13)

## Research problem
{: #problem }

Conventional undersampling patterns are often designed to support the reconstruction of an entire velocity field. A smaller inverse problem asks for a few boundary-condition parameters that affect that field, such as distal outlet resistances. A frequency that contributes visibly to a velocity image may still give little evidence about which resistance changed. The authors ask which subset of the raw complex MRI measurements carries the most information about the target parameters, given a fixed number of sampling locations. They compare the resulting designs with a central circular mask and a pseudo-random Gaussian mask. [Preprint, Introduction and Sections 2.2–2.3, PDF pp. 1–5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06712v1#page=1)

## Core contributions
{: #innovation }

1. **Design the mask around the parameters of interest.** Perturb the flow parameters, simulate the corresponding changes in phase-contrast MRI measurements, and score each mask by how well the retained measurements distinguish the perturbations.
2. **Use the same observation model for design and estimation.** After selecting the mask, compare the forward model with the sampled complex k-space data in a reduced-order unscented Kalman filter. The estimated quantities are model parameters such as the distal resistances.
3. **Test discrete designs against simple alternatives.** Compare a sequential greedy algorithm with a restricted exchange algorithm, and check what happens when the sensitivities used to design the mask come from a poor initial estimate. The analytical and aortic experiments expose different weaknesses of the sampling patterns. [Preprint, Sections 2–4, PDF pp. 2–23](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06712v1#page=2)

## Method
{: #method }

### From outlet resistance to an MRI measurement

A three-element Windkessel model represents each downstream vascular bed using a proximal resistance, a distal resistance and a compliance. The parameters set the outlet pressure–flow relationship for a three-dimensional incompressible Navier–Stokes solver. To simulate phase-contrast MRI, the velocity is mapped to a regular grid, encoded as phase using the specified velocity-encoding constant, combined with the given magnetization magnitude and background phase, and Fourier transformed. The binary mask retains selected k-space locations. At each time point, the filter compares the retained complex data with predictions from the current parameter estimates; it can update the resistance estimates using those residuals directly. [Preprint, Equations (3)–(8), (17)–(19) and Appendix B, PDF pp. 2–3, 13–15, 27–29](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06712v1#page=2)

### How does a frequency help tell two parameters apart?

Let <span class="literature-math">μ<sub>S</sub>(θ)</span> stack the real and imaginary parts of the predicted samples selected by mask <span class="literature-math">S</span> over time. For a trial change <span class="literature-math">δθ</span>, its first-order signal is <span class="literature-math">J<sub>S</sub>δθ</span>, where <span class="literature-math">J<sub>S</sub></span> is the Jacobian at the design point. With Gaussian noise covariance <span class="literature-math">Σ</span>, the squared, noise-weighted amplitude of that signal gives the Fisher-information quadratic form. Stacking real and imaginary parts expresses Equation (14) in a real-valued form.

If two resistance changes produce nearly identical patterns over the sampled frequencies, increasing one resistance and decreasing the other can leave only a small residual. The corresponding parameter direction has little information. Picking a frequency that responds differently to the two changes can improve that weak direction even if its measured signal is small. For an identifiable local model, A-optimality minimizes <span class="literature-math">tr(F<sup>−1</sup>)</span>, the sum of approximate marginal variances. D-optimality minimizes <span class="literature-math">det(F<sup>−1</sup>)</span>, which shrinks the associated uncertainty ellipsoid. These scores depend on the assumed noise and the model used to compute the derivatives. A-optimality also depends on how the parameters are scaled. [Preprint, Equations (9)–(15) and optimality criteria, PDF pp. 4–5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06712v1#page=4)

The greedy algorithm starts with an empty mask and adds the best available frequency for the current design criterion at each step. A small diagonal term allows the algorithm to evaluate masks before their information matrix becomes invertible. The exchange algorithm starts with a randomly initialized mask of the required size, then swaps selected frequencies with nearby unused ones while the score improves. Both build one feasible design at a time. The exchange search can revisit a choice that the greedy algorithm has committed to, at a runtime that depends on its starting mask and the number of swaps. [Preprint, Algorithms 1–2, PDF pp. 5–6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06712v1#page=5)

## Experiments and results
{: #experiments }

### When does sampling the center miss useful information?

The analytical example combines constant and linear terms with a sinusoid of known frequency 7. In Figure 2, look for the center peak for the polynomial coefficients and the two off-center peaks at <span class="literature-math">k = (0, ±7)</span> for the sinusoidal coefficient. The center has almost no sensitivity to that coefficient. The Gaussian and circular masks shown in Figure 8 miss those peaks. With 30 Gaussian noise realizations at the reported SNR of 15, the designed masks better recover the sinusoidal coefficient, while the conventional masks can fare better on some polynomial coefficients at small budgets. The filter started at the true parameters for this benchmark. The experiment isolates the effect of selecting informative frequencies under favorable initialization. [Preprint, Section 3, Figs. 2 and 8–10, PDF pp. 7–11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06712v1#page=7)

### How much does the mask help in the simulated aorta?

The authors simulated 56 velocity frames on a 2 mm grid and applied a 2D frequency mask repeated through the third dimension and across velocity components. They used first-order finite differences with relative step <span class="literature-math">10<sup>−6</sup></span> for the design. Inflow sensitivity is much larger and more centered than the three distal-resistance sensitivities, so the masks target the three resistances. The filter used 50 independent complex Gaussian noise realizations at the reported SNR of 15 and ten passes per realization. The three resistances were initialized to 4,000 each; their simulated true values were 7,200, 11,520 and 11,520, in the paper’s units. [Preprint, Sections 4.2–4.4 and 4.7, PDF pp. 15–19](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06712v1#page=15)

Start with the five-location group in Figure 22. All four designed masks have a much lower total relative error than the Gaussian and central circular baselines there. At 25 and 50 locations, the circular mask is close to the designed masks, and the Gaussian mask still has the larger mean error. Across budgets, the five-location exchange estimates beat the 50-location Gaussian baseline on mean error, while the circular mask in the nominal-50 group uses 49 points and has a lower mean error than the five-location exchange masks. The comparison depends on the baseline. The mean exchange error also rises between budgets 10 and 25. Section 4.7 suggests that the nonlinear filter may converge to a different local solution, which remains a possible explanation. [Preprint, Figs. 20 and 22, PDF pp. 18–20](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06712v1#page=18)

In the sensitivity test, replacing the true design parameters with the filter’s initial guesses left many masks and errors similar. An important exception was the A-optimal greedy mask at budget five, whose error rose noticeably. Compare the two masks in Figure 27 and their errors in Figure 26b. The test supports the approach under one alternate set of design parameters and shows that at a tight budget even a small change in the selected frequencies can matter. [Preprint, Section 4.8 and Figs. 26–27, PDF pp. 20 and 23](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06712v1#page=23)

### How long does the optimizer take?

For the three-resistance design at budget five, Table 3 lists 9.32 s for greedy selection and 10 s for exchange. At budget 50, the times are 4 min 34 s and 2 h 18 min. Converting those reported values to 274 s and 8,280 s gives an exchange-to-greedy runtime ratio of about 30 for that setting. These are computation times for designing the mask on the paper’s setup. Differences in implementation, starting masks and model dimensions can change that ratio. [Preprint, Section 4.4 and Table 3, PDF pp. 15–16](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06712v1#page=16)

## Conclusions and limitations
{: #conclusions }

A mask designed for the target parameters can use the available samples more efficiently in this numerical setting. The advantage depends on the budget, the comparison mask and the sensitivity model used for design. The study’s approximately tenfold contrast in sampling locations concerns the flow-encoding directions and selected comparisons. The experiments used simulated acquisitions, a single aortic geometry, known magnetization magnitude and background phase, and Gaussian noise. Patient coils, calibration, background-phase variation and scanner sequence constraints all affect how many useful samples can be acquired in a measured scan time. [Preprint, Discussion and Conclusion, PDF pp. 23–25](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06712v1#page=23)

## Future directions
{: #future }

The authors propose testing coil-sensitive observations, uncertain background phase and designs that account for uncertain nominal parameters. A direct next test would fit the sensitivity model on one set of geometries and evaluate masks with a sequence-constrained, equal-time acquisition on independent geometries or flow phantoms. Reporting error for each resistance, alongside measured scanner time, would show when the local information score predicts a practical benefit. [Preprint, Discussion, PDF pp. 23–24](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06712v1#page=23)

Electrical impedance tomography offers a related test. One could choose drive and sensing pairs to distinguish an inclusion’s conductivity from electrode contact impedance at the same acquisition time. Compare a criterion averaged over plausible contact impedances with a single-nominal-point design and a fixed baseline on held-out phantoms. Predefine the target contrast and localization errors and have an evaluator score the reconstructions without knowing which protocol acquired the data. This is a proposed transfer of the measurement-design idea. [Preprint, local information design and uncertainty discussion, PDF pp. 4–5 and 23–24](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06712v1#page=4)
