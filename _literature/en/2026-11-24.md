Two small arteries can look similar on a vascular map and carry very different shares of the blood reaching a downstream branch. To see this distinction at the circle of Willis, Nabil Haidour and colleagues tracked individual ultrasound contrast microbubbles through the intact skull in two macaques. The acquisition recorded data for 279 volumes per second, which the system used for tracking after reconstruction. It combined trajectories collected over three minutes in macaque H and two minutes in macaque M to build the vascular maps. The volumetric frame rate describes how frequently the tracker could look for each moving bubble. Spatial detail in the complete map also depends on how many bubbles have traveled through a vessel. The study reports imaging up to 7 cm deep over a volume on the order of 2.5 × 3.5 × 4 cm³. [Published paper, Abstract and Methods, PDF pp. 1 and 12](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-026-03314-4.pdf#page=12)

## Research problem
{: #problem }

The circle of Willis connects the anterior and posterior cerebral circulations. A small communicating artery may provide an alternative path when pressure changes, but shape alone gives limited information about how blood actually moves through a junction. Time-of-flight MR angiography provides a useful anatomical comparison. Volumetric flow MRI and transcranial Doppler provide complementary information with different costs in temporal sampling, coverage and sensitivity to small vessels. For ultrasound there is an additional measurement problem: the skull attenuates and distorts the pulses used to detect targets centimeters beneath it. The authors wanted to recover both the course of the vessels and the velocity and direction of flow through them. [Published paper, Main, PDF pp. 1–2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-026-03314-4#Sec1)

## Core contributions
{: #innovation }

1. **Adapt the probe and transmission to the skull.** A wide matrix array with individual diverging lenses and a conformable fluid interface improve coupling. The team estimates the local skull orientation, evaluates transmission windows, then uses phase-coded emissions from a subset of the array.
2. **Recover long trajectories from volumetric ultrasound.** After compensating acoustic delays and suppressing tissue echoes, the system finds individual bubbles and links their positions over repeated volumes. Long tracks help follow trajectories across branches and retain velocity and direction.
3. **Measure several distinct quantities from those trajectories.** The paper distinguishes velocity profiles, local separability of neighboring velocity samples and spatial resolution estimated from accumulated density. Track fractions also provide a way to describe how flow is distributed at a bifurcation. [Published paper, Fig. 1 and workflow, PDF pp. 2–3](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-026-03314-4#Fig1) · [Extended Data Fig. 5](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-026-03314-4#Fig10)

## Method
{: #method }

### From coded echoes to a trajectory

The transmitter uses echoes from an estimated 5–7 cm depth interval to select an acoustic window. The system sends a sequence of 20 Hadamard-coded emissions with a 1 MHz transcranial probe. It decodes and combines the echoes into one volume, using propagation delays for the lens, coupling fluid, skull and brain. The 5,580 Hz pulse repetition rate divided by 20 gives 279 reconstructed volumes per second, about 3.58 ms between volumes. The skull thickness used for reconstruction was assumed constant at 4–5 mm, consistent with the MRI measurements in this setup. An interframe linking limit of 1.07 mm controls matching across volumes. The distance limit times the volume rate gives a frame-to-frame matching scale of about 299 mm/s. Fast trajectories can be missed near or above this scale. [Published paper, Methods, PDF pp. 11–12](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-026-03314-4#Sec17)

Spatiotemporal singular-value filtering suppresses tissue signals so individual bubble echoes can be localized. The tracker links the detected centers over time. Tracks shorter than five frames are rejected, and streamline rendering and flow-splitting analyses retain tracks longer than 5 mm. As a result the mapped velocities and branch fractions describe the retained trajectories. At a bifurcation, the estimated fraction for one branch is the count of retained tracks that enter it divided by the total retained count across the relevant branches. Its interpretation depends on maintaining similar detection and tracking efficiency across branches. [Published paper, Methods, PDF pp. 12–13](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-026-03314-4#Sec19) · [Flow-splitting analysis](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-026-03314-4#Sec22)

### Which quantities come from a flow model?

A bubble’s changing position supports a local velocity estimate. The wall shear stress map adds a model. The authors estimate the vessel radius from the reconstructed centerline geometry and compute wall shear stress under a Poiseuille relation, using dynamic viscosity <span class="literature-math">μ</span>, flow rate <span class="literature-math">Q</span> and estimated radius <span class="literature-math">R</span>.

For steady, fully developed laminar flow in a circular tube, a parabolic velocity profile gives this relation between flux and the velocity gradient at the wall. The paper uses the maps primarily for relative spatial variation. A radius error matters because the radius appears cubed for a given flow rate, and the reconstruction’s own flux estimate also depends on the radius. Curvature, branching and pulsatility can make the pipe-flow approximation less representative. [Published paper, Eq. ## Experiments and results
{: #experiments }

### Can neighboring positions in a small vessel show different velocities?

Figure 3 illustrates this test in the posterior communicating arteries in H and the P2 segment of the posterior cerebral artery in M. First use the highlighted branches in panels c and d to see where the authors extracted each profile. In e and h, the horizontal axis marks position across the vessel and the vertical axis shows bubble velocity. Look for higher central velocities and a decline toward the edges. A two-sided Student’s t-test compared selected neighboring voxels spaced 151 μm apart in H and 191 μm apart in M. The figure reports 54–777 tracked bubbles per voxel across the two profiles in H, and 6–60 in M. These are samples from the velocity profiles of the two animals. The resulting spacings describe this local test and are conditional on the vessel, velocity contrast, sampling density and tracking selection. ### Is that the same as spatial resolution of the vascular map?

In Fig. 5d the authors divided odd and even tracks into two density reconstructions and used the half-bit Fourier shell correlation (FSC) threshold. This measures how finely the accumulated maps agree in three dimensions. The reported estimates were about 163 μm in H and 156 μm in M. For anatomy, compare the gray 3D time-of-flight MRA maps with the red ultrasound overlay in Fig. 5a–b. Major branches broadly align, and some small vessels depicted by ultrasound are absent from this specific 0.5 mm isotropic MRA-TOF acquisition. The MRI and ultrasound maps were overlaid manually. This is a descriptive anatomical comparison, so branch sensitivity and absolute velocity accuracy would require separate reference measurements. The contrast-enhanced power Doppler comparison likewise shows a qualitative gain in branch delineation under a similar accumulation time. [Published paper, Fig. 5 and Methods, PDF pp. 8 and 12–13](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-026-03314-4#Fig5) · [Extended Data Fig. 8](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-026-03314-4#Fig13)

### Do the two animals show the same flow distribution?

In Fig. 4a, follow the tracks leading to the left and right P2 segments in H. The left posterior communicating artery accounted for 61% of the tracks contributing to the left P2 segment, whereas the right counterpart contributed 9% to the right P2 segment. In M, the basilar artery accounted for more than 90% of the estimated contribution to each P2 segment. These fractions use trajectories longer than 5 mm. In the paper’s bifurcating tube phantom, the reference outlet split shown in Extended Data Fig. 6a was 31%/69%. The track-based estimates in panel e were 32%/68% as central values, providing a controlled check for that geometry and flow setting. The comparison between H and M illustrates individualized patterns. The sample consists of two anesthetized male macaques of different ages and species, and gives no estimate of how frequent either pattern is in a population. Thousands of tracks within one animal improve sampling of that animal’s vessels. [Published paper, Fig. 4 and Methods, PDF pp. 6–7 and 12–13](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-026-03314-4#Fig4) · [Extended Data Fig. 6, PDF pp. 21–22](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-026-03314-4#Fig11)

## Conclusions and limitations
{: #conclusions }

The experiment demonstrates deep, transcranial tracking and anatomical mapping of the circle of Willis in two macaques. It provides examples of distinct flow partitioning through similar territories and resolves small vessels under the tested conditions. Skull curvature and aperture size limited coverage. Tissue motion can blur small structures, and weak or rapidly moving bubbles can be harder to retain. The track-based branch fractions and derived wall shear stress maps require further validation against independent measurements, especially for geometry, flow and motion conditions beyond those in the study. [Published paper, Discussion, PDF pp. 8–9](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-026-03314-4#Sec9)

## Future directions
{: #future }

The paper proposes motion correction, alternative probe configurations and dynamic ultrasound localization microscopy for better coverage and evaluation of pulsatile flow. A practical follow-up would compare retained-bubble fractions with independently metered branch flows in a phantom while varying branch radius, flow speed and the acoustic window. Reporting the fraction of tracks retained in each branch would help identify whether apparently asymmetric flow is accompanied by asymmetric detection. A parallel test in electrical impedance tomography could vary electrode contact impedance in a perfused phantom and estimate whether a conductivity or flow-related change is supported by channels withheld from reconstruction. Both tests would examine whether the observed pattern survives a change in how the signal is acquired.
