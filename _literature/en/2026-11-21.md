Several images with different fine details can agree with the same undersampled MRI data. A small structure synthesized by a model can look plausible in an otherwise sharp reconstruction. QUTCC provides a map of interval widths alongside the image so readers can identify locations where a second look might help. In one fastMRI example, both methods create a structure absent from the reference and assign a wide interval in that region.

This note uses the 30-page public v3 PDF, including the appendix, by Cassandra Tong Ye, Shamus Li, Tyler King, and Kristina Monakhova. The paper was selected from the November 18 digest. ## Research problem
{: #problem }

Undersampling leaves some spatial frequencies unobserved. Even within one image, the measurement can constrain the background and fine structures to different degrees. Im2Im first predicts pixel-specific intervals, then scales both sides with a single image-wide factor. The initial intervals already vary by location, but calibration can only rescale them together. K-RCPS assigns separate factors to predefined pixel groups. The paper asks whether calibration can make intervals follow the local error distribution more closely at the same target miscoverage rate. [Manuscript, §§2.1–3, PDF pp. 3–5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.14760v3#page=3)

## Core contributions
{: #innovation }

1. **One network for multiple quantiles.** The quantile level is an extra input sampled over a continuous range during training. The same attention U-Net returns a median reconstruction, a lower quantile image, or an upper quantile image.
2. **Calibration in quantile space.** Calibration selects a shared pair of query levels. How much the interval moves at each location depends on the learned quantile curve there. One pixel’s upper side can widen substantially while another changes very little. The lower and upper levels are calibrated separately.
3. **Intervals and densities from the same model.** Two queries give an interval. More queries, followed by numerical differentiation, give a pixel-wise conditional density estimate. Estimating a density costs more and depends on the shape and accuracy of the learned quantile curve. [Manuscript, §3, PDF pp. 5–6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.14760v3#page=5)

## Method
{: #method }

### How does the network use a quantile level?

The model takes an aliased image <span class="literature-math">y</span> and a quantile level <span class="literature-math">q</span>. Sinusoidal encoding and a small perceptron embed the level in a U-Net with self-attention. Training samples <span class="literature-math">q</span> at random and uses it to condition both the network and its loss. At <span class="literature-math">q = 0.95</span>, underpredicting by 0.1 costs 0.095, while overpredicting by the same amount costs 0.005. That asymmetry pushes the learned estimate toward an upper quantile. Querying <span class="literature-math">q = 0.5</span> gives an estimate of the conditional median. ### Why allocate half the miscoverage budget to each tail?

For the lower side, compute the fraction of reference pixels below the lower bound in each calibration image, then average across images. Repeat for reference pixels above the upper bound. If a tail exceeds its budget, move its query level outward. Equation (5) splits the target into two budgets of <span class="literature-math">α/2</span>, with a finite-sample adjustment used in the search. Let <span class="literature-math">X</span> be a randomly selected reference pixel and <span class="literature-math">L</span> and <span class="literature-math">U</span> its predicted endpoints. Write the resulting interval as <span class="literature-math">I = [L, U]</span>.

The union bound controls the total risk once each tail is controlled. It works even when the two events are dependent. A population guarantee also depends on exchangeable calibration and test units and valid calibration of an ordered interval family. Pixels within an image are correlated and do not supply that many independent cases. The paper targets pixel-marginal coverage. Simultaneous coverage for a whole image or coverage of a particular anatomical region calls for a separate risk definition and validation. [Manuscript, Eq. (5), Algorithm 1 and §5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.14760v3#page=5) · [Conformal Risk Control](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02814)

One implementation detail matters. Page 5 applies the finite-sample adjustment separately to each tail, while Algorithm 1 on page 6 adjusts the total budget before halving it. These produce different thresholds. The public implementation follows Algorithm 1. The experiments below report empirical risks. A strict finite-sample guarantee requires the appropriate threshold and sampling assumptions. [Manuscript, PDF pp. 5–6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.14760v3#page=5) · [Authors’ calibration code](https://github.com/cassandra-t-ye/QUTCC-UQ/blob/main/evaluation/calibration.py#L110-L158)

Binary search also assumes that the fitted quantile increases with its query level. Sharing weights helps, and the paper still measures occasional crossings. The authors evaluate a repair that uses the pixel-wise minimum and maximum as endpoints during calibration and testing. They can fall back to a grid search when binary search becomes unstable. In Table 6, the five tasks return the same calibration levels with and without the repair. [Manuscript, Appendix A.3.3–A.3.4 and Tables 5–6, PDF pp. 18–19](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.14760v3#page=18)

## Experiments and results
{: #experiments }

### How much shorter are the fastMRI intervals?

The authors split NYU fastMRI into 700 training, 200 calibration, and 200 validation volumes, each with multiple slices. Appendix A.6.3 describes a 2D Fourier transform, an undersampling mask, and an inverse transform to construct aliased inputs from fully sampled slices. Table 2 separately compares four acceleration factors. The main comparisons set the target miscoverage rate to 0.10. [Data and masks, PDF p. 26](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.14760v3#page=26) · [Table 2, PDF p. 8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.14760v3#page=8)

| Acceleration | Im2Im | QUTCC | Reduction |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 4× | 0.1121 | 0.1116 | 0.45% |
| 8× | 0.1371 | 0.1348 | 1.68% |
| 12× | 0.1530 | 0.1511 | 1.24% |
| 16× | 0.1690 | 0.1643 | 2.78% |
{: .literature-results }

These are the interval lengths reported in the paper. The reduction spans 0.45%–2.78%, with the largest gain at 16× and a dip from 8× to 12×. A separate appendix ablation isolates the calibration step. Switching the QUTCC model to Im2Im-style calibration yields an MRI interval length of 0.1071, slightly shorter than the 0.1083 in Appendix Table 8 for QUTCC. The ranking reverses for Poisson denoising, with lengths of 0.0482 and 0.040, respectively. The calibration gain depends on the task’s error distribution. [Appendix A.3.2, PDF p. 18](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.14760v3#page=18) · [Table 8, PDF p. 20](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.14760v3#page=20)

### What do runtime and empirical risk add to the comparison?

In Appendix Table 8, empirical MRI miscoverage is 0.099 for QUTCC and 0.098 for Im2Im-Deep, near the common 0.10 target. The MRI row of Table 9 reports 61.67 ms for QUTCC, 35.30 ms for Im2Im-Deep, and 359.14 ms for the deep ensemble. QUTCC queries the median and the two endpoints separately. Flexible quantile queries come with a higher latency than Im2Im-Deep in this setup. [Appendix Tables 8–9, PDF p. 20](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.14760v3#page=20)

## Conclusions and limitations
{: #conclusions }

QUTCC generates a reconstruction and pixel-wise intervals from one network. Its interval reductions over Im2Im at the four fastMRI accelerations are modest. Some Poisson denoising comparisons show a larger effect where uncertainty varies more with intensity. Narrower intervals alone do not establish better MRI reconstruction. Appendix A.3.1 reports similar reconstruction metrics, and both models invent the feature in Figure 4. QUTCC needs paired references, and uncertainty can be underestimated for rare structures or out-of-distribution inputs. [Manuscript, §5, PDF p. 10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.14760v3#page=10) · [Appendix A.3.1, PDF p. 17](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.14760v3#page=17)

## Future directions
{: #future }

An electrical impedance tomography follow-up could calibrate conductivity intervals on phantoms with known inhomogeneities. Split training, calibration, and testing by phantom or acquisition batch, then vary voltage noise and electrode contact impedance independently. Measure interval width and miscoverage over the full domain, in target interiors, and at boundaries. If overall coverage is adequate but misses concentrate on small targets, test a target-specific risk. If errors increase after electrode changes while intervals stay narrow, test whether the original calibration data represent the new measurement conditions. These are proposed EIT tests motivated by the paper’s scope. [Coverage and distribution shift, PDF p. 10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.14760v3#page=10)
