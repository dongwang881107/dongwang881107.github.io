Imagine reconstructing a CT image from noisy projections at just 50 angles. Two reconstructions might fit those data about equally well, even if one has lost small structures inside the object. A coordinate network adds a question of its own. Can the network represent those structures in the first place?

Palumbo, Massa and Benvenuto choose **which images the network can represent** alongside **how strongly the solution is pulled toward a reference image**. The paper gives conditions for adjusting both as the noise falls. In simulated CT, the network preserves some outer boundaries clearly while fine detail can still be smoothed away. Published online November 16, 2026, in *SIAM Journal on Numerical Analysis*. Selected from the November 18 curated digest. [Publisher’s article page](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/25M1762774) · [Authors’ open manuscript v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19936v1)

## Research problem
{: #problem }

In grid-based Tikhonov reconstruction, increasing α pulls the solution closer to the reference image. With a coordinate network, the reconstruction must also belong to the class of images that the network can represent, even at small α. If that class cannot express a feature, reducing the measurement noise leaves that source of error in place. The paper asks how quickly the admissible class should expand and α should decrease as measurements become cleaner. [Authors’ open manuscript, §§2.1–2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19936v1#S2)

## Core contributions
{: #innovation }

1. **A second regularization parameter.** The paper treats the index of a compact class of admissible solutions as a parameter alongside the Tikhonov weight. Approximation error from that class appears explicitly in the analysis.
2. **A construction for coordinate networks.** With an architecture and bounded weights, the outputs form a compact class. A suitable family of such networks with nonnegative outputs can approximate a broader function space.
3. **A joint parameter choice.** The paper relates the penalty and representation error to noise. Under the appropriate source conditions and approximation control, the method can attain the classical order in the linear setting. [Authors’ open manuscript, Theorems 2.2, 2.5–2.6 and 3.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19936v1#S2.SS3)

## Method
{: #method }

Let x denote the image, y<sup>δ</sup> the noisy data, and F the physical operator that predicts measurements. The reconstruction minimizes the following objective over the m-th compact set Xₘ. [Authors’ open manuscript, Eq. (6)](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19936v1#S2.SS2)

<p class="literature-equation">min<sub>x ∈ Xₘ</sub>  ‖F(x) − y<sup>δ</sup>‖² + α‖x − x*‖²</p>

### How do the projections become an image?

1. **Generate a candidate image.** The network maps each spatial coordinate to an intensity. A final ReLU enforces nonnegativity. The compact set Xₘ describes the current class of admissible images.
2. **Predict what the scanner would measure.** Apply the forward model F to the candidate. The first term measures disagreement with the observed projections. The second penalizes departure from the reference image x*, with weight α.
3. **Update the network using those measurements.** Fit the network separately for each set of observations and read the reconstruction from the fitted network. The numerical result also depends on the initialization and can be affected by local minima. [Authors’ open manuscript, §3.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19936v1#S3) · [Numerical implementation, §4.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19936v1#S4)

### How should the two parameters change as noise falls?

As the noise δ falls, α should decrease while δ²/α tends to zero. The index m must also make the squared approximation error of the compact class vanish relative to α. The first ratio controls the influence of noise relative to the penalty. The second controls the cost of using a restricted representation. Under the source condition for the linear setting and sufficient approximation accuracy, the paper obtains the classical order-optimal rate. [Authors’ open manuscript, §§2.2–3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19936v1#S2.SS3)

## Experiments and results
{: #experiments }

### Figure 2. Do small features survive along with the outline?

The authors simulated standard Radon projections of a 128 × 128 Shepp–Logan phantom at 50 angles and added white Gaussian noise. They used Adam to fit a multilayer perceptron with four hidden layers of 100 units each, separately for each observation. [Authors’ open manuscript, §§4.1–4.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19936v1#S4)

Start with the middle row at 23.10 dB. The right column shows the noisy projections. The left and middle columns reconstruct those same data with the network and Tikhonov, respectively. The network’s outline is cleaner, and the small features near the bottom of the phantom are still hard to distinguish. The three rows run from about 42.60 dB at the top to 16.58 dB at the bottom. The comparison reflects both the image representation and the constraints. The closed-form Tikhonov baseline searches the full grid space, while the network enforces nonnegativity. [Authors’ open manuscript, Fig. 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.19936v1#page=18)

### Supplementary Figure SM68. How does the network compare with two baselines under attenuation?

The supplement extends the comparisons to Shepp–Logan and walnut images, standard Radon and the authors’ attenuated model, and three methods. The baselines are nonnegative Tikhonov and a Plug-and-Play (PnP) method with a DnCNN denoiser. The full settings use 25 noise realizations per condition at 20, 25 and 30 dB. The authors report ℓ₂ error, PSNR and SSIM. [Journal supplement, §§SM10–SM12](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/suppl/10.1137/25M1762774/suppl_file/supp_mat.pdf)

The redraw uses the **five displayed noise realizations** for attenuated Radon and a walnut image at **20 dB** from Fig. SM68. Compare the three methods along a single row to hold the noise realization fixed. Farther left means lower ℓ₂ error in the top panel. Farther right means higher structural similarity (SSIM) in the bottom panel. Mean ℓ₂ errors in these five cases are 14.62 for Tikhonov, 11.91 for PnP and 10.80 for the coordinate MLP. The MLP has lower error and higher SSIM in each of those pairs. The complete supplement gives the variation across the full set of runs and conditions. [Journal supplement, Fig. SM68, printed p. SM94](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/suppl/10.1137/25M1762774/suppl_file/supp_mat.pdf#page=94)

## Conclusions and limitations
{: #conclusions }

**What the theory supports.** Under the stated convergence conditions, the solution class and penalty can be chosen together, with compact-set approximation error governing how accurately the class must represent the solution. [Authors’ open manuscript, §§2.2–3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19936v1#S2.SS3)

**What the experiments leave open.** Lower overall error in simulation can coexist with smoothing of fine detail, and network variability grows at higher noise. The supplementary experiments use known ground truth to select parameters. Real measurements still call for a data-driven selection rule and a check for sensitivity to forward-model mismatch. [Authors’ open manuscript, §§4.2–5](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19936v1#S5) · [Journal supplement, §SM10](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/suppl/10.1137/25M1762774/suppl_file/supp_mat.pdf)

## Future directions
{: #future }

The authors suggest evaluating more object morphologies. For electrical impedance tomography, three checks would make that question concrete. [Authors’ open manuscript, §5](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19936v1#S5)

1. **Separate noise and model mismatch.** Compare grid-based Tikhonov and a constrained coordinate network on the same simulated conductivity phantoms. Vary voltage noise separately from electrode-position and contact-impedance errors.
2. **Select both parameters on equal measurements.** Estimate noise from repeated measurements. Use held-out excitation patterns to select the network constraint and penalty weight, and reserve a separate set of excitation patterns to report test voltage error.
3. **Evaluate conductivity and small targets.** Record held-out voltage error, conductivity bias in the target region, and boundary recovery. If voltage prediction improves while conductivity bias persists, investigate identifiability and forward-model error.
