A lung electrical impedance tomography (EIT) monitor can display a waveform with every breath. To convert that waveform’s amplitude into milliliters, a reader needs to know how much volume a device-specific impedance unit represents. Vincent Joussellin and colleagues paired expiratory volume measurements with EIT in 120 intubated patients from four French intensive care units. They found that the conversion factor varies both across patients and within short recordings from the same patient. [Publisher pre-proof, abstract and Results, manuscript pp. 2–3 and 10–12](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aicoj.2026.100210)

## Research problem
{: #problem }

A chest EIT belt provides a reconstructed cross-section from boundary electrical measurements. Air moving in and out of the lung changes the impedance, and the summed waveform records the resulting variation. The same airway volume may produce a different waveform amplitude if the air redistributes between regions, the diaphragm moves relative to the belt, or the reconstructed signal is normalized to a different baseline. These are plausible mechanisms raised by the authors. The available recordings do not identify their individual contributions. The measurement question is whether a single scale can convert an impedance swing into an expired tidal volume across patients and whether a patient-specific scale holds from one breath to the next. [Publisher pre-proof, Introduction and Discussion, manuscript pp. 4–5 and 13–16](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aicoj.2026.100210)

## Core contributions
{: #innovation }

1. **Evaluate conversion at two levels.** The study calculates one average calibration factor per patient and analyzes its variation across patients within each device cohort. It separately quantifies breath-to-breath variation within each patient.
2. **Test whether clinical measurements explain the scale.** The authors fit separate exploratory models for the two EIT cohorts and use the fitted scales to estimate tidal volume. BMI is the strongest reported univariate correlate in both cohorts.
3. **Evaluate agreement in volume units.** The authors compare the converted volumes against the airway reference via Bland–Altman analysis. This links variability in the calibration factor to the size of individual volume errors. [Publisher pre-proof, Methods and Results, manuscript pp. 7–12](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aicoj.2026.100210)

## Method
{: #method }

This is a retrospective secondary analysis of prospectively collected data from six parent studies. Patients were intubated, had a <span class="literature-math">PaO₂/FiO₂</span> ratio below 300 mmHg, and had synchronized volume and impedance measurements. The PulmoVista 500 was used at three sites with 16 electrodes. The Enlight 1800 was used at one site with 32 electrodes. Recordings lasted 5–20 minutes under the parent protocols, with differences in the breaths available for analysis. The airway reference came from the ventilator or a pneumotachograph. For the latter, the team aligned peaks and checked lags of up to five breaths in either direction. [Publisher pre-proof, Methods, manuscript pp. 5–8](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aicoj.2026.100210)

### What does the calibration factor divide?

For patient <span class="literature-math">i</span> and breath <span class="literature-math">b</span>, <span class="literature-math">ΔZ<sub>ib</sub></span> is the maximum minus the minimum of the global reconstructed impedance signal during that breath. The signal uses all reconstructed pixels, including negative contributions. The paper labels its ratio “TIV/VT”, while its calculation divides measured expiratory tidal volume by the impedance swing. Here <span class="literature-math">g<sub>ib</sub></span> denotes the reported conversion in mL/AU. The patient factor averages the individual breath ratios. [Publisher pre-proof, Calibration Methods, manuscript pp. 7–8](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aicoj.2026.100210)

The distinction between two coefficients of variation (CV) matters. For a device cohort, the between-patient CV takes the standard deviation divided by the mean of the patient factors <span class="literature-math">ḡ<sub>i</sub></span>. Each patient’s within-recording CV applies the same formula across that patient’s breath factors <span class="literature-math">g<sub>ib</sub></span>. The results report the median and interquartile range of those patient-level CVs. Scaling the standard deviation by the mean gives a percentage in both cases. [Publisher pre-proof, Statistical Analysis, manuscript pp. 8–9](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aicoj.2026.100210)

### How did the authors test an estimate without individual calibration?

The authors first screened clinical variables in each device cohort, then selected a multivariable linear model with stepwise Akaike information criterion (AIC). To limit overlap between height, weight and BMI, they admitted only the one with the strongest univariate fit. The model supplies an estimated scale <span class="literature-math">ĝ<sub>i</sub></span>, which they multiply by the current <span class="literature-math">ΔZ<sub>ib</sub></span>. The resulting volumes enter a Bland–Altman comparison with airway measurements. The limits of agreement describe the spread of differences in the analyzed cohort, while the mean bias describes their average. [Publisher pre-proof, Statistical Analysis and Table 2, manuscript pp. 8–9 and 29](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aicoj.2026.100210)

## Experiments and results
{: #experiments }

### How much do the scales differ across patients?

The paper reports 17,884 analyzed breaths from 120 patients. In the 74-patient PulmoVista cohort, the median scale was **0.17 mL/AU** (IQR 0.14–0.26) and the between-patient CV was **98%**. In the 46-patient Enlight cohort, the corresponding values were **22.8 mL/AU** (IQR 17.7–26.7) and **40%**. The two device pipelines report different arbitrary units. The dispersion within either cohort directly bears on whether a cohort-wide scale can be used for an individual. The two devices were used on different patients, so the median contrast cannot isolate a hardware or reconstruction effect. [Publisher pre-proof, Results, manuscript pp. 10–11, and Fig. 1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aicoj.2026.100210)

### How stable is the scale over a single recording?

The median within-patient CV was **10%** across the full sample (IQR 5–23%). As panel A below shows, the cohort medians were 20% for PulmoVista and 5% for Enlight. The early ARDS, transition and extubation-day groups had medians of 5%, 40% and 15%, respectively. These strata describe different patients. Each recording was short, so the stage comparison cannot establish how an individual patient's calibration changes over a course of ventilation. All Enlight patients were in the early ARDS phase under volume-controlled ventilation, while 52 of 74 PulmoVista patients were recorded on the day of extubation. The within-patient result is therefore strongest as evidence about short-term variation. [Publisher pre-proof, Results and Discussion, manuscript pp. 10–11 and 16–17, Fig. 2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aicoj.2026.100210)

### Does regression make the estimated volumes agree?

BMI alone accounted for <span class="literature-math">R² = 0.31</span> of the PulmoVista factor variance and <span class="literature-math">R² = 0.40</span> of the Enlight factor variance in the univariate analyses. The final models reached adjusted <span class="literature-math">R²</span> of **0.36** and **0.63**, respectively. The PulmoVista model retained BMI, plasma protein concentration and hematocrit. The Enlight model retained BMI, airway resistance, temperature, hematocrit and heart rate. A predictor can be retained by the AIC procedure with a p-value above 0.05, as occurred for PulmoVista hematocrit and Enlight heart rate. The models provide exploratory associations and were evaluated using the study’s own measurements. [Publisher pre-proof, Results, manuscript pp. 11–12 and Table 2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aicoj.2026.100210)

In panel B, compare the width of the intervals with the positions of the dots near zero. For PulmoVista, mean bias was **+0.86 mL/kg PBW** with limits **−7.69 to +9.40**. For Enlight, mean bias was **−0.33 mL/kg PBW** with limits **−3.35 to +2.70**. The median measured volume in the full sample was 6.1 mL/kg PBW. Estimates and reference volumes therefore differed enough for a small average bias to be misleading about any particular reading. The difference also grew with the mean volume in the proportional-bias analysis. The factor models’ adjusted <span class="literature-math">R²</span> and the agreement limits answer different questions. The regression statistic describes variance explained in calibration factors. The limits put the volume errors on a milliliters-per-kilogram scale. [Publisher pre-proof, Results, manuscript pp. 10–12](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aicoj.2026.100210)

## Conclusions and limitations
{: #conclusions }

The authors infer that absolute tidal volume estimation requires patient-specific calibration. The observed breath-to-breath variation then limits the reliability of a single fitted factor, with larger variability observed in the pressure support group. EIT’s relative impedance changes remain meaningful observations under a documented setup. This study did not prospectively verify the error of applying a patient-specific factor to later recordings. [Publisher pre-proof, Discussion and Conclusions, manuscript pp. 13–18](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aicoj.2026.100210)

The comparison is observational, with device, center, ventilation mode and disease stage intertwined. The airway reference used two measurement approaches. Recording windows and analyzable breath counts varied with the parent protocols. The study also lacked measurements that would separate baseline normalization changes from changes in the physiological signal gain. Findings apply to the sampled intubated population and the acquisition conditions examined. [Publisher pre-proof, Methods and Limitations, manuscript pp. 5–8 and 15–17](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aicoj.2026.100210)

## Future directions
{: #future }

- **Test when a calibration expires.** Estimate a patient-specific factor on a prespecified block of simultaneous EIT and airway measurements, freeze it, and assess later held-out breaths. Compare a fixed factor and a periodically updated factor using absolute error, proportional bias and coverage of the prediction intervals. Repeat across changes in posture, mode or effort, and keep all of a patient’s recordings together in external evaluation splits.
- **Separate acquisition scaling from physiological variation.** A same-patient device comparison with a common airway reference would reduce case-mix effects. In a controlled bench stage, identical volume changes with a fixed geometry and logged raw boundary measurements could isolate changes introduced by reconstruction and normalization. Prospective measurements of effort and belt position would then help investigate patient-level variation.
- **Make uncertainty visible.** An EIT inverse pipeline can report a relative signal or a volume estimate with an uncertainty interval conditioned on the calibration data. Predictive intervals can be tested on held-out patients and later recording blocks. The paper’s combination of modest average bias, wide limits of agreement and proportional bias gives a concrete reason to evaluate all three quantities. [Study limitations and proposed cross-over, manuscript pp. 15–17](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aicoj.2026.100210)
