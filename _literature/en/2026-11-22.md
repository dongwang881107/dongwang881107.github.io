An axon can disappear from a wiring diagram if a section goes missing or its membrane blurs in depth. In mouse cerebellar tissue, Kuan and colleagues used electron tomography to look inside thicker sections and reduce the number of physical cuts. A single-axis series of 21 projections from −30° to +30° preserved enough detail to identify chemical synapses and other fine structures in selected views. The price of that faster sampling is direction-dependent blur, which matters most when tracing structures through depth.

The paper appeared in *Nature Methods* on November 19, 2026 and was selected from the November 21 curated literature digest. The acquisition and numerical comparisons below use the authors’ 19-page bioRxiv v1 PDF, posted June 6, 2024. The main journal PDF is subscription-access. The separate tests on directional resolution and learned restoration are identified as results from the journal’s public Extended Data. ## Research problem
{: #problem }

Conventional serial-section transmission electron microscopy acquires images of many thin physical sections. Thicker sections reduce the number of interfaces where a process can become difficult to follow, but a single projection superimposes structures at different depths. Electron tomography resolves depth by acquiring projections at multiple tilts, each with its own acquisition and positioning cost. The authors ask which combination of tilt range, number of exposures and magnification preserves the structures needed for circuit mapping, with the option to reimage a site of interest at higher resolution. [Preprint, Introduction and Results, pp. 1–4](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.06.05.597487v1.full.pdf#page=1)

## Core contributions
{: #innovation }

1. **Measure the angular sampling trade-off.** The authors reconstruct the same tissue region from subsets of a dense acquisition, varying angular span and projection count separately. This makes it possible to distinguish the effect of an extra view from the effect of extending angular coverage.
2. **Use semi-thick sections at two scales.** Align consecutive low-magnification tomograms to follow structures in three dimensions, then place higher-magnification tomograms of selected regions back into that volume. This concentrates the cost of dense multi-axis acquisition on structures needing a closer look.
3. **Test effects on tracing and segmentation.** The preprint follows a specific axon across 15 sections. The published Extended Data adds direction-specific resolution measurements and compares raw limited-tilt, restored limited-tilt and fully sampled inputs for affinity prediction on a held-out volume. [Preprint, Figs. 1–2, pp. 2–6](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.06.05.597487v1.full.pdf#page=2) · [Published Extended Data Figs. 1 and 7](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-026-03345-x#Sec18)

## Method
{: #method }

### From electron projections to a three-dimensional volume

The input is a series of two-dimensional electron micrographs of the same field of view at known specimen tilts. The authors align the micrographs, reconstruct each section by filtered back-projection in most experiments, and use weighted iterative reconstruction for the volume in preprint Figure 2. To register neighboring tomograms, they estimate section orientation and locate the surfaces, then fit in-plane affine transformations to corresponding surfaces. Composing those transformations places adjacent sections in a common coordinate system. [Preprint, Methods, pp. 7–10](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.06.05.597487v1.full.pdf#page=7)

In a simplified parallel-beam model, let <span class="literature-math">f</span> denote the three-dimensional image, <span class="literature-math">R<sub>θ</sub></span> the tilt rotation and <span class="literature-math">s</span> the coordinate along the beam. Each detector value integrates the rotated object along a line. This equation explains the sampling geometry. Thick specimens can also degrade the measured signal through scattering and attenuation.

The Fourier slice theorem associates each projection with a plane through the three-dimensional Fourier transform. A limited single-axis tilt range leaves a wedge of frequencies unsampled. Increasing the projection count fills more planes inside the existing span. Widening the span shrinks the missing wedge, but at fixed count the angular gaps grow. For 21 evenly spaced views including both endpoints, ±30° gives a 3° step, and ±60° gives a 6° step. A wider span therefore changes both coverage and density, while a thicker specimen sends fewer useful electrons to the detector at high tilts. [Preprint, Results and Methods, pp. 3–4 and 7–9](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.06.05.597487v1.full.pdf#page=3)

### Why compare two rotation axes?

The preprint estimates resolution using Fourier shell correlation (FSC), a normalized agreement measure between corresponding Fourier coefficients. Its test volumes use a subset of one tilt series, and the reference uses 481 projections from the orthogonal tilt series. The authors reverse the roles of the axes, average the comparisons, and use a one-bit threshold to locate the resolution cutoff. A split into even and odd views on the same axis would share a missing wedge and could inflate agreement. Orthogonal axes change the artifact orientation, but a single shell average can still hide the difference between in-plane and depth resolution. The test views also share alignment estimated from the dense series. Independent alignment using 21 raw views remains a separate test. [Preprint, FSC and alignment, pp. 8–10](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.06.05.597487v1.full.pdf#page=8)

## Experiments and results
{: #experiments }

### How much do 21 views recover?

In preprint Figure 1c, read across the 21-view row to compare angular spans for a 750-nm section. Then read down the ±30° column to compare counts. Examine the orthogonal slices as well as the central image, since blur can be easier to see through depth. In Figure 1d the authors identify unmyelinated neurites, chemical synapses, gap junctions and spinule-like structures in selected limited-tilt reconstructions. The richer reference in that panel also uses higher magnification. The visual differences reflect both acquisition settings. It uses four axes with 241 views each, or 964 views in all. The ratio 964/21 is about 46, a comparison of projections in those two setups. Mechanical movement, exposure, focusing and section handling all contribute to elapsed time. [Preprint, Fig. 1c–d and Results, pp. 2–4](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.06.05.597487v1.full.pdf#page=2) · [Published Supplementary Video 1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-026-03345-x#MOESM4)

Preprint Figure 1e–f reports that with 21 projections over ±30° at 300 kV, the FSC estimates are 11.8, 12.7 and 14.8 nm at 3,300× for nominal section thicknesses of 500, 750 and 1,000 nm. At 11,000× the corresponding estimates are 4.2, 4.4 and 4.6 nm. Within each magnification, thicker sections give a larger resolution value, meaning coarser resolution on this metric. At 3,300×, the preprint uses seven tomograms across the three thicknesses. At 11,000× it uses three tomograms, one per thickness. Variability from chunks within the same tomogram should be read as within-sample variability. [Preprint, Fig. 1e–f and Results, pp. 2–4](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.06.05.597487v1.full.pdf#page=4) · [Sample counts, p. 10](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.06.05.597487v1.full.pdf#page=10)

### Does favorable average resolution guarantee faithful depth detail?

The published Extended Data Figure 1 measures the spread of 5-nm gold fiducials along different directions. Compare the in-plane XY and cross-sectional XZ views in panel b, then the fitted profiles in panel c and the elongation ratios in panels e and i. The widths quantify how a tiny object spreads differently across directions. The published figure also gives a 13.7-nm example from an in-plane FSC curve using a half-bit threshold. The public peer-review file records a change for the journal version to comparing two limited-tilt volumes on orthogonal axes with a half-bit threshold, in place of v1’s dense-reference comparison at one bit. Its in-plane example and the v1 three-dimensional estimates above measure agreement under different conditions. To assess whether a process can be traced through depth, consider the direction-specific fiducial measurements as well. [Published Extended Data Fig. 1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-026-03345-x#Fig3) · [Preprint, FSC limits, p. 9](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.06.05.597487v1.full.pdf#page=9) · [Authors’ response to peer review, PDF p. 19](https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41592-026-03345-x/MediaObjects/41592_2026_3345_MOESM3_ESM.pdf#page=19)

### Can an axon be followed across physical sections?

The authors aligned fifteen 600-nm sections into a volume of about 10 × 10 × 9 µm. Preprint Figure 2 traces an example granule-cell parallel-fiber axon and shows higher-magnification views of selected features in the same region. The preprint Methods specify that the example manual segmentation used the fully sampled version of the stack. The journal’s Supplementary Video 7 compares the axon across the 21- and 241-view series directly. In a separate experiment, published Extended Data Figure 7 shows that restoration with a Pix2Pix-inspired model improves affinity prediction relative to raw limited-tilt inputs on one held-out test volume. Affinity curves measure a step in segmentation. Verification of individual junctions would require structure-level error analysis, including false positives. [Preprint, Fig. 2 and Methods, pp. 5–6, 10–11](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.06.05.597487v1.full.pdf#page=5) · [Published Extended Data Fig. 7](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-026-03345-x#Fig9) · [Published Supplementary Video 7](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-026-03345-x#MOESM10)

## Conclusions and limitations
{: #conclusions }

The experiments identify an operating point for the tested mouse cerebellar sections. They also show what a multi-scale workflow can do when a valuable region can be reimaged. The published supplementary table estimates imaging times for 1 mm³ on one microscope. Its three electron-tomography workflows range from 893 to 2,000 days, versus 1,043 days for the modeled GridTape serial-section TEM comparison. These are estimates from event counts and assumed times. Changing the order of tilts and tiles affects their totals substantially even though the number of projections per series stays at 21. A measured end-to-end throughput claim would require an automated acquisition at scale, with section loss, alignment failures and repeat imaging included. [Published Supplementary Table 1, PDF p. 3](https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41592-026-03345-x/MediaObjects/41592_2026_3345_MOESM1_ESM.pdf#page=3)

## Future directions
{: #future }

A prospective acquisition experiment could hold the time budget fixed while changing tilt span and density as separate factors. A dense or multi-axis reference on independent fields would allow blinded readers to score missing and spurious synapses, membrane continuity through depth and section-boundary errors. This would test when the speed gain is worth the loss of particular details. [Published imaging data and reconstruction code](https://github.com/htem/tem-tomo)

Electrical impedance tomography has a related experimental-design question. With the same budget of electrode measurements, one could compare adding nearby, partly redundant voltage channels with adding drive and sensing patterns that interrogate different directions or depths. A phantom study with known inclusions could measure target detection, contrast error and sensitivity to contact-impedance changes. The aim would be to choose measurements for the structures one needs to recover, then check that choice against independent data. [Preprint, sampling and evaluation design, pp. 8–10](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.06.05.597487v1.full.pdf#page=8)
