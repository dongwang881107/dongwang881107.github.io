Imagine a ring of ultrasound transducers around a breast phantom. Each transmitter sends a wave through the phantom, and receivers pick up the scattered field. If most transducers are unavailable, or the array covers only one side, different sound-speed maps can give rise to much the same data. Xiang Cao and colleagues use a learned prior to propose a map, then use the measured acoustic field to correct it. Their simulated sparse-view reconstruction took 1.1 seconds per sample on the reported GPU setup. A comparison holding the initializer fixed shows that refinement with the conventional wave solver can still produce a more accurate map. ## Research problem
{: #problem }

The sought image is the spatial sound speed. The observations are complex-valued pressures sampled at the receivers. Changing the speed changes the phase of the scattered wave, so minimizing the residual can lead the search toward the wrong phase alignment, a problem known as cycle skipping. Fewer sensors around a full ring leave gaps in spatial sampling. Sensors confined to an arc leave gaps in angular coverage and produce different artifacts. A prior can rule out implausible maps, but each physics correction still needs a new wave prediction for the current speed estimate. [Sections 1.1–1.2, pp. 1–3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16344v2#page=2)

## Core contributions
{: #innovation }

1. **Condition a fast generative prior on a preliminary inversion.** An encoder-decoder called Inversion-Net maps the observations to a rough image. A trainable control branch passes this image to a frozen consistency model, which estimates a clean image from a noisy state in a few steps. Training the conditional model requires paired simulated measurements and sound-speed maps. [Fig. 2 and Section 4.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16344v2#page=8)
2. **Reuse predicted wavefields for the measurement gradient.** The formulation uses coincident source and receiver positions. Under the reciprocal Helmholtz formulation of the paper, an adjoint field can be assembled from the batch of forward fields. A multigrid neural operator (MgNO) predicts that batch at each correction. [Eqs. (12)–(15) and Fig. 3, pp. 7–8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16344v2#page=7)
3. **Generate wavefield supervision during training.** The authors sample plausible sound-speed maps from the consistency model and generate reference wavefields with a batched convergent Born series solver (BCBS). The trained operator is subsequently evaluated on relatively clean image estimates during inversion. This links the distribution of its training inputs to its intended use. [Algorithm 1, p. 9, and Section 5.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16344v2#page=9)

## Method
{: #method }

### From received pressures to a sound-speed image

Inversion-Net converts the observed source-by-receiver matrix into the conditioning image. A conditional consistency model combines that image with a noisy state to propose a clean speed map. Diff-ANO interpolates the map from the 256 × 256 image grid to the 480 × 480 physics grid and predicts the wavefields. It updates the map to reduce the residual at the actual receivers, brings the result back to the image grid and adds controlled noise before the next consistency step. The alternating process produces a point reconstruction. The authors choose five sampling times near the clean end of the trajectory, from 0.10 to 0.18. [Algorithm 2, p. 10, and Section 6.1, p. 13](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16344v2#page=10)

### Why one batch of wave predictions can guide the update

Let <span class="literature-math">X</span> be the real sound-speed map and <span class="literature-math">A(X)</span> the predicted receiver data. At fixed angular frequency <span class="literature-math">ω</span>, the forward field solves a Helmholtz equation with coefficient <span class="literature-math">ω²/X²</span>. If <span class="literature-math">J<sub>X</sub></span> differentiates that data map, then differentiating the squared complex residual in a real direction gives twice the real part of the inner product of the residual with the predicted data perturbation. Moving the derivative to the other side of the inner product gives the real-valued gradient of the squared residual <span class="literature-math">T</span>. [Eqs. (1), (12)–(15), pp. 1 and 7–8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16344v2#page=7)

Applying <span class="literature-math">J<sub>X</sub><sup>*</sup></span> corresponds to injecting residual-dependent sources at the receiver positions. Linearity in the source lets the authors form the response by superposing fields from those positions. Reciprocity lets them reuse the coincident transmitter batch in that construction. With complex wavefields, the adjoint construction must account for conjugation. Substituting MgNO predictions affects both the estimated receiver residual and the fields that weight it. Forward error alone does not measure the resulting gradient error. [Eqs. (13)–(15) and Section 7.3, pp. 7–8, 19–20](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16344v2#page=7) · [Journal DOI](https://doi.org/10.1088/1361-6420/aec045)

## Experiments and results
{: #experiments }

### When sensors are missing, which method gives the most faithful map?

For the OpenBreastUS benchmark, the authors used 2,700 training and 300 test fibroglandular (FIB) phantoms, and 1,800 training and 200 test extremely dense (EXD) phantoms. The four acquisition settings use a 220 mm ring. Sparse I and II keep 64 and 32 source–receiver positions around the full ring. Partial I and II keep 64 positions over a quarter circle and 32 over an eighth circle. The main simulations use 500 kHz with noise-free, 10 dB and 5 dB SNR conditions. Table 2 gives a single PSNR/SSIM pair per geometry and method. Its caption refers to three noise levels, but the table leaves open which level or aggregation each pair represents. It gives no uncertainty across repeated training runs. [Sections 5.1 and 6.3, Fig. 6 and Table 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16344v2#page=16)

The conventional CBS/L-BFGS setup uses 500 inner wave-solver iterations and 30 outer steps. Diffusion Posterior Sampling (DPS) uses 1,000 steps and retains the best result from trial step sizes 0.2, 0.1 and 0.05. The Decomposed Diffusion Sampler (DDS) uses 50 steps and conjugate-gradient updates. Two standalone supervised methods are the Neural Inverse Operator (NIO) and Inversion-Net. Both Diff-ANO variants in Table 2 use five adjoint updates with a fixed step size of 0.1. The conditional Tweedie variant uses a conditional mean estimate for the clean image, while the conditional consistency variant uses the learned consistency map. [Sections 6.1 and 6.3, pp. 13–16](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16344v2#page=13)

Look first at Partial II, where both the number of transducers and angular coverage are reduced. Inversion-Net reaches 26.17 dB and SSIM 0.8960. Conditional Tweedie with the neural adjoint reaches 28.44 dB and 0.9329, and the conditional consistency variant reaches 29.51 dB and 0.9510. Moving from Tweedie to the consistency map raises the printed PSNR by 1.07 dB with the same adjoint and step size. For Partial I, the same subtraction gives 0.17 dB. These differences were calculated from the two-decimal PSNR values printed in the table. [Table 2, p. 16](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16344v2#page=16)

In the second row of Fig. 7 at 5 dB SNR, compare the small structures in the EXD ground truth with the same positions in the DPS, DDS and Diff-ANO columns. DPS reproduces the broad outline and misses much of the internal pattern. The second row in Fig. 8 shows pronounced directional artifacts for CBS under partial coverage. In Fig. 9, compare the first two reconstructions of the first Sparse II example. One neural-adjoint update lowers PSNR from 26.09 to 26.06 dB, even though the test averages in Table 1 rise as updates are added. An average gain leaves room for individual failures. [Fig. 7, p. 14](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16344v2#page=14) · [Figs. 8–9 and Table 1, pp. 15–16](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16344v2#page=15)

### How much accuracy does the fast wave surrogate give up?

In the Sparse I comparison, the initializer stays fixed and the operator used for refinement changes. Relative forward wavefield error drops from 0.0882 for the Fourier neural operator (FNO) to 0.0264 for the larger MgNO. Image PSNR rises from 29.58 to 32.07 dB. Using CBS for refinement raises it to 32.85 dB and raises SSIM from 0.9732 to 0.9803. Read the last two entries in the inversion rows of Table 7 together. Their printed PSNR difference of 0.78 dB measures the accuracy cost of replacing the solver in this setting. Table 3 gives whole-pipeline timings for Sparse I, including 1.1 s for Diff-ANO, 298.5 s for GPU CBS and 451.9 s for DDS. These pipelines have different optimization procedures, so the timings do not isolate the speedup due to the wave surrogate. MgNO training alone took approximately 130 A100 GPU-hours. [Table 3, p. 17](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16344v2#page=17) · [Table 7, p. 19](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16344v2#page=19)

## Conclusions and limitations
{: #conclusions }

In the simulations reported in v2, the image prior supplies sound-speed estimates that measurement updates generally improve, while MgNO cuts the time spent predicting waves. Table 6 shows how much performance depends on the starting image. In Sparse II, replacing a one-step gradient initializer with Inversion-Net raises the complete pipeline from 23.76 to 30.24 dB with MgNO held fixed. The experiments in v2 leave open sensitivity to real transducer response and discrepancies between two-dimensional and three-dimensional propagation. Paired training for the conditional model and the structure needed for adjoint reuse also limit direct transfer. [Table 6 and Section 7.3, pp. 19–20](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16344v2#page=19)

## Future directions
{: #future }

The authors propose learning the forward and adjoint operators separately for problems where reciprocity cannot support the same reuse. For ultrasound tomography, a follow-up could hold the initializer, acquisition and update count fixed, then compare the surrogate and a reference solver on measurements from independent simulators and physical phantoms. Wavefield error, gradient alignment and the residual on withheld sensors would each reveal a different failure mode. [Section 7.3, pp. 19–20](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16344v2#page=19)

For electrical impedance tomography, the transferable idea is the combination of a prior and a verified measurement correction. A controlled test could use the same voltage data and initializer for exact finite-element and surrogate gradients under a complete electrode model. Keep target geometry fixed, vary one electrode’s contact impedance, and compare the direction and magnitude of conductivity updates against the exact solver. Then introduce a real target while holding contact stable. These two cases would test whether the learned update sharpens genuine conductivity contrast while limiting false targets generated by electrode drift.
